Lesson 3

Planning a flight

  • Level: Intermediate
  • Reading time: about 12 minutes
  • SimBrief · Fenix A320

A real flight starts at the desk long before the engines are running. Before takeoff, pilots receive a flight plan (OFP) with the route, fuel and weights, and study it carefully. In the sim, the best tool for that is SimBrief. In this lesson we'll plan an A320 flight from Istanbul (LTFM) to Antalya (LTAI).

The diagrams and sample values are for teaching. Real values depend on the aircraft profile, the load and that day's winds.

1What is SimBrief?

SimBrief is a free website that produces flight plans very similar to the ones real airlines use. You log in with your Navigraph account. You can use it without a Navigraph subscription too; the only difference is that the navigation data is a slightly older cycle. What matters is that the data in SimBrief matches the navigation data of your aircraft in the sim.

2Flight details and aircraft

When you start a new flight plan in SimBrief, you first enter the basics: the flight number (for example VWC001), the ICAO codes of the departure and arrival airports (LTFM and LTAI) and the departure time.

Then choose the aircraft type: A319, A320 or A321. You can save your own aircraft profile in SimBrief, which stores the registration, engine type and weight limits. If you build a profile that matches the engine type and weights of the aircraft you fly in the Fenix, your plan will be much more realistic and you won't have to set it up again for every flight.

3Route

You can type the route by hand, but at first I'd recommend SimBrief's route finder; it suggests routes that are actually flown between those airports. You can also leave the cruise altitude and the alternate on automatic. SimBrief works out the best altitude for the winds and the aircraft's weight.

One note: SimBrief's route usually doesn't include the departure (SID) and arrival (STAR) procedures. We'll pick those in the MCDU before the flight, based on the runway in use and ATC's instructions.

The Cost Index (CI) is set here too. This number says whether you value time or fuel more. A low CI means flying slower on less fuel; a high CI means flying faster on more fuel.

4Fuel: What makes up the block fuel?

This is the most important part of the lesson. The total fuel you put in the tanks is called block fuel, and every component has a job.

T
TaxiFuel burned on the ground before takeoff. At big airports like Istanbul, taxi times can be long.
Y
TripFuel burned from takeoff to landing at the destination. This is the heart of the plan.
E
ContingencyFor the unexpected, usually 5% of the trip fuel. The wind may differ from the forecast, or ATC may route you the long way round.
A
AlternateThe fuel needed to divert to the alternate airport (Dalaman, LTBS, in the example) if you can't land at the destination.
R
Final reserveFuel that lets you hold for another 30 minutes at the alternate. On a normal flight it's never touched; if it becomes clear you'll land with less than this, the pilot declares a fuel emergency (MAYDAY FUEL).
+
ExtraFuel added at the captain's discretion, for example when bad weather or heavy traffic is expected. Zero in the example.
Cenk's tipDon't think "the more fuel, the safer". More fuel means more weight; the aircraft burns more and you might bump into the landing weight limit. Take the fuel the plan tells you, and add extra only for a reason.

5Weights and limits

Passengers, baggage and cargo determine the aircraft's weight. When you enter the passenger count and cargo, SimBrief calculates three key weights. Each has an upper limit set by the aircraft's structural strength.

Z
ZFW (zero fuel weight)The aircraft, passengers, baggage and cargo; everything except fuel. If you exceed the limit, cutting fuel won't help; you have to reduce the load.
T
TOW (takeoff weight)ZFW plus the fuel on board at takeoff (block fuel minus taxi fuel). In the example: 60,500 + 5,440 = 65,940 kg.
L
LW (landing weight)Takeoff weight minus trip fuel. In the example: 65,940 − 2,800 = 63,140 kg.

6Generate and read the OFP

When everything's ready, generate the plan. SimBrief gives you a long document called the OFP (operational flight plan). It contains the route, the wind and temperature at every waypoint, the fuel calculation, the weights, the airport weather and NOTAMs. Don't be put off; at first it's enough to look at these three sections:

1
Flight summaryCruise altitude, Cost Index and estimated elapsed time (EET). The route is written here too.
2
Fuel planThe components we described above and, most importantly, BLOCK: the total fuel you'll put in the tanks.
3
WeightsLists the planned weights next to their limits. Every planned value must be below its limit.
Cenk's tipGenerate the plan just before the flight. SimBrief uses the current winds and weather. If live weather is on in the sim, your plan and the sky will match, and so will your fuel figures.

7Importing the plan into the Fenix

Thanks to the Fenix's SimBrief integration, you don't need to type the plan in by hand. You enter your SimBrief username once in the settings on the Fenix's cockpit tablet (EFB). After that:

Route: Go to the INIT page on the MCDU and press INIT REQUEST. The departure, arrival and route load automatically. Then always check the F-PLN page for any gaps in the route (DISCONTINUITY).

Fuel and load: From the loading page on the EFB you can load the fuel and payload from your SimBrief plan into the aircraft. When loading is complete, check that the values on the EFB match the OFP.

Menu names may differ slightly depending on your Fenix version. We'll do the SID, STAR and the rest of the MCDU step by step in Lesson 4, starting from a cold & dark cockpit.

✓Planning checklist

Pre-flight planning
  • Departure, destination and alternate correct
  • Aircraft profile matches your aircraft in the Fenix
  • Block fuel calculated and looks sensible
  • ZFW, TOW and LW below their limits
  • Airport weather checked
  • Route imported into the MCDU, no discontinuities

?Test yourself

Click a question to see the answer.

What components make up the block fuel?

Taxi, trip, contingency, alternate, final reserve and, if any, extra fuel.

Your ZFW comes out at 63,000 kg and the limit is 62,500 kg. Can you fix it by taking less fuel?

No. ZFW doesn't include fuel, so cutting fuel changes nothing. You need to reduce the cargo or the load.

When is the final reserve fuel used?

Normally never. It's the last safety margin that must stay in the tanks under all circumstances. If it becomes clear you'll land with less than this, the pilot declares a fuel emergency.

What happens if you increase the Cost Index?

The aircraft flies faster and arrives earlier, but burns more fuel.

ZFW 60,500 kg, block fuel 5,640 kg, taxi fuel 200 kg. What's your takeoff weight?

60,500 + (5,640 − 200) = 65,940 kg. Taxi fuel is burned before takeoff, so it's not included in the takeoff weight.
